Two Safe Ways to Earn on Your Cash — But They Work Differently

When it comes to keeping your money safe while still earning a return, Australian savers generally have two main options sitting at the conservative end of the spectrum: high interest savings accounts and term deposits. Both are straightforward, both are government-protected up to a point, and both beat leaving money idle in an everyday transaction account. But they suit different situations, and understanding the difference can mean more money in your pocket without taking on any extra risk.

How High Interest Savings Accounts Work

A high interest savings account (sometimes called a HISA) is a deposit account offered by banks and online lenders that pays a higher rate of interest than a standard savings or transaction account. Your money stays accessible (you can deposit more or withdraw funds whenever you need) while still earning interest on your balance.

Most high interest savings accounts in Australia come with a catch: the advertised rate is often a bonus rate, meaning it only applies if you meet certain monthly conditions. Common requirements include depositing a minimum amount each month (say, $200 or $1,000), making no withdrawals, or linking the account to an eligible everyday account with the same bank. If you miss the conditions, you revert to a much lower base rate.

Interest is typically calculated daily and paid monthly. Because the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) sets the official cash rate, and banks broadly follow it, HISA rates tend to move up or down after each RBA decision.

Bonus rate conditions matter. Some accounts advertise an attractive headline rate, but if you miss a single condition in a month, you could earn a fraction of that rate. The base rate is your fallback, so it helps to know it.

How Term Deposits Work

A term deposit is a fixed-term agreement with a bank or authorised deposit-taking institution (ADI). You lock in a lump sum for a set period (anything from one month to five years) and the bank guarantees a fixed interest rate for the entire term. When the term ends (the maturity date), you receive your original deposit back plus the interest earned.

The trade-off is inflexibility. Unlike a savings account, you generally can't access your money before the term ends without paying a break fee or reduced interest penalty. That's why people tend to use term deposits for money they won't need in a hurry.

Interest can be paid monthly, quarterly, annually, or at maturity depending on the product and term length. For longer terms, having interest paid periodically rather than at the end can be useful for cash flow, and it's something people often factor into their comparison.

Current Rate Context in Australia

Following the RBA's rate hiking cycle that began in May 2022, both savings account and term deposit rates rose significantly from the record lows of the pandemic era. At their peak, competitive HISAs were offering rates above 5% per annum, and term deposits for 12-month terms were sitting in a similar range for many of the major banks and online lenders.

As the RBA began cutting rates in 2025, these returns started softening. The key point is that rates aren't permanent. A HISA rate can change at any time with little notice, while a term deposit locks in whatever rate you agreed to at the start. This dynamic is central to the choice between them.

For current rates, ASIC's MoneySmart website is a useful starting point for unbiased comparisons, as is checking directly with banks and online lenders such as ING, Macquarie, Ubank, and the major four banks (ANZ, CBA, NAB, Westpac).

Flexibility vs Rate: The Core Trade-Off

Why some people prefer a savings account

  • You're building an emergency fund and need access at short notice
  • You're saving towards a goal with an uncertain timeline (e.g. a house deposit where settlement could vary)
  • You're receiving regular income you want to park temporarily
  • You expect interest rates to rise further and want to benefit from any increases
  • You prefer the ability to make additional deposits over time

Why some people prefer a term deposit

  • You have a lump sum you won't need for a defined period
  • You want certainty — a guaranteed rate regardless of what the RBA does next
  • You expect rates to fall and want to lock in today's higher rate
  • You're a retiree or near-retiree who values predictable income
  • You want to remove the temptation to dip into savings

Some people use both: three to six months of living expenses in a flexible HISA for emergencies, with surplus cash in a term deposit once the buffer is set. Others keep everything in one savings account for simplicity, or spread money across several term deposits so one matures every few months.

The Government Deposit Guarantee

Both high interest savings accounts and term deposits held with Australian ADIs (banks, building societies, credit unions) are covered by the Financial Claims Scheme (FCS), administered by the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA). This government guarantee protects deposits up to $250,000 per person, per institution.

If you have more than $250,000 in cash savings, spreading deposits across multiple institutions means each balance is separately protected. This matters most for retirees, those who have recently sold a property, or anyone holding a large cash balance temporarily.

What Inflation Does to Your Savings

Here's the part that often gets overlooked. Even if a term deposit is paying 4.5% per annum, what matters is your return after inflation. If inflation is running at 3.5%, that's only around 1% before tax.

The Australian Taxation Office (ATO) also requires you to declare interest income as part of your assessable income each financial year. At a marginal tax rate of 30% (plus the Medicare levy), a 4.5% rate can leave you with an after-tax, after-inflation return that barely keeps pace with living costs. That's why some people use cash mainly for short-term money and look to other assets for long-term growth. Others value the certainty of cash enough to accept the lower return.

The Bigger Picture: Where Cash Fits

Cash savings serve a vital purpose: they keep your money safe, keep it easy to get to, and reduce anxiety. But history shows that over the long run, assets like Australian shares (ASX-listed equities), diversified managed funds, property and, importantly for most Australians, superannuation have tended to deliver higher returns after inflation than cash deposits, with more ups and downs along the way.

Superannuation in particular offers tax-advantaged compound growth over decades. Over the long run, money in super has often grown faster than cash in even the best-paying savings account, though it's locked away until preservation age. That's why many people use savings accounts and term deposits mainly for short-term goals, emergency reserves and keeping money safe.

Cash saving and investing aren't either/or. Many people hold both: accessible cash for near-term needs and stability, alongside growth-oriented investments for the long term.

Putting It Together

High interest savings accounts and term deposits are both legitimate, low-risk tools for Australian savers. Savings accounts offer flexibility and ease. Term deposits offer rate certainty and a bit of built-in discipline. It often comes down to how soon you might need the money, and whether instant access matters more to you than locking in today's rate.

Things to weigh up

  • When might you need this money, and how sure are you of that date?
  • Would a break fee or reduced interest matter if plans changed?
  • Do you think rates are more likely to rise or fall, and how much does that matter to you?
  • Can you reliably meet a savings account's bonus conditions each month?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.

Cash keeps your money safe while you decide what's next. Investing aims for long-term growth, with more ups and downs along the way.
