The whole appeal of concessional super contributions is the tax gap between your marginal rate and the 15% rate applied inside super. Division 293 exists to narrow that gap for high earners, on the reasoning that the tax concession is worth proportionally more to people already on the top marginal rates.

The threshold and the extra tax

If your income plus your concessional super contributions for the year exceed $250,000, an additional 15% tax applies to the portion of your contributions above that threshold — effectively lifting the tax rate on those contributions from 15% to 30%.

This $250,000 threshold is set in legislation and hasn't been indexed since it was reduced from $300,000 in 2017 — meaning as wages grow over time, more people gradually fall into Division 293 territory without the threshold moving.

Worked example
Sam earns a $230,000 salary in 2026–27. Their employer pays 12% super guarantee on top: $27,600. That already uses most of the $32,500 concessional cap, so Sam can only salary sacrifice the remaining $4,900.

Salary sacrifice lowers Sam's taxable income to $225,100, and total concessional contributions become $32,500. Division 293 income is the two added together: $225,100 + $32,500 = $257,600, which is $7,600 over the $250,000 threshold. The extra 15% applies to that $7,600, a Division 293 bill of $1,140.

Notice the sacrifice didn't change that figure. Without it, Sam's income would be $230,000 plus $27,600 of employer super: the same $257,600. Salary sacrifice moves money from one side of the sum to the other, so the $4,900 still goes in at 15% instead of Sam's 47% marginal rate (45% plus Medicare).

Is it still worth salary sacrificing?

On the tax maths, it usually still comes out ahead. Even with Division 293 pushing the effective rate to 30% on the affected portion, that's still meaningfully lower than the top marginal rate of 45% plus the 2% Medicare levy. For someone on the top bracket whose contributions are caught by Division 293, the net benefit of salary sacrificing drops from roughly 32 cents in the dollar (47% − 15%) to about 17 cents in the dollar (47% − 30%). That's smaller, but still a real benefit. And as the example shows, if you're only a little over the threshold, the extra tax is worked out on the amount you're over, not on every dollar you put in.

What counts towards the $250,000

"Division 293 income" is a specific calculation — broadly your taxable income, plus reportable fringe benefits, plus net investment losses, plus low-tax contributions (your concessional contributions, minus any excess). It isn't just your gross salary, so someone with large employer super contributions, investment losses or fringe benefits might trigger Division 293 at a lower headline salary than expected. With 12% super guarantee, a salary of about $223,000 is enough on its own.

How and when it's charged

You don't pay this at the time contributions are made — the ATO issues a separate Division 293 assessment after you lodge your tax return, once your full income picture for the year is known. You can choose to pay it from your own funds or have it released from your super balance.

The bigger picture

Division 293 narrows the concession but doesn't remove it. For most people near the threshold, salary sacrificing up to the cap still comes out ahead on tax — the saving is reduced but still meaningfully positive, and the money is still compounding inside a lower-tax environment than it would outside super.
