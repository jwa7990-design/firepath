If you've got a few debts on the go, the question of which one to tackle first comes up fast. Some people pay a bit extra on each one. Many prefer to pick one target and put everything spare towards it, so one debt disappears sooner. If you go that way, the question is which one.

There are two well-known ways to choose. Both get people there. They suit different people.

The ground rule: minimum payments on every debt

Both methods assume at least the minimum keeps being paid on every debt. Missing a payment brings late fees, can show up on your credit report, and on a credit card can mean losing an interest-free period. Any extra money then goes to one debt at a time.

The avalanche: highest interest rate first

With the avalanche method, people list their debts by interest rate and put every spare dollar on the one with the highest rate. Once it's gone, you roll that payment onto the next-highest rate, and so on down.

Some people prefer it because, on the maths, it costs the least in interest. Every extra dollar goes where it saves the most. Paying down a 20% credit card saves 20% interest on that money, guaranteed. The catch is that the highest-rate debt can also be a big one, so the first win can take a while.

The snowball: smallest balance first

With the snowball method, people set the rates aside and go after the smallest balance first. Clear it, roll that payment onto the next-smallest, and keep going.

Others prefer it because a whole debt disappears sooner, and seeing one go can make it easier to keep going. It usually costs a bit more in interest. How much more depends on how far apart the rates are.

A worked example

Worked example Mia, 31, is a dental nurse in Adelaide. She has three debts and can put $1,000 a month towards them in total:

• Credit card: $8,000 at 20%, minimum $240 a month
• Personal loan: $3,000 at 13%, minimum $100 a month
• Car loan: $14,000 at 9%, minimum $350 a month

That's $690 in minimums, leaving $310 a month extra to aim at one debt. When a debt's cleared, its payment rolls onto the next one, so she's always paying $1,000 a month until she's done.
Avalanche (card first) Snowball (personal loan first)
First debt cleared Credit card, month 17 Personal loan, month 8
All debts cleared Month 29 Month 29
Total interest paid About $3,770 About $3,990

The avalanche saves Mia about $220 in interest. The snowball gets her first win nine months sooner. Both have her debt-free in under two and a half years, because the big thing driving the result is the $1,000 a month, not the order.

The gap between the two methods grows when the high-rate debt is also the biggest, or when the rates are far apart. It shrinks when the rates are close.

Common misconception

"Paying the minimum keeps a credit card under control." On Mia's $8,000 card, a typical minimum of about 2.5% of the balance (falling as the balance falls) would take around 25 years to clear and cost close to $14,000 in interest, assuming she never used the card again. Minimums are set low on purpose.

Where common debts usually sit

Credit cards

Usually the most expensive debt people carry, often around 20% a year. They tend to sit at the top of an avalanche list. If you're carrying a balance, some people move it to a lower-rate or 0% balance transfer offer. That can help, but only if the balance is cleared before the offer ends and the old card isn't run up again.

Personal loans and car loans

These have fixed repayments and usually lower rates than cards, though unsecured personal loans can still be in the teens. Check whether paying extra or paying out early attracts a fee, since some loans have them.

Buy now pay later

Buy now pay later (BNPL) splits a purchase into instalments, often with no interest. The cost comes from late fees and account fees if you miss a payment. A single small BNPL balance is easy to manage. Several at once are easy to lose track of, so some people clear these early just to simplify things.

HECS/HELP

Your HELP debt (the student loan formerly called HECS) isn't charged interest. Instead it's indexed once a year on 1 June, by the lower of the inflation rate (CPI) or wage growth (WPI). Repayments come out through the tax system once your income passes a threshold, which for 2026–27 is $69,528, and they're based on income, not the balance.

Because indexation is usually lower than other debt rates and is capped at wage growth, HELP usually sits near the bottom of an avalanche list. Voluntary repayments are allowed. Some people make them before 1 June to reduce indexation, or because they like having it gone. Others leave it to the tax system and put spare money elsewhere. In dollar terms, the indexation saved is usually smaller than the interest saved on a credit card balance of the same size.

The mortgage

Home loans usually carry the lowest rate of any debt, and they run for decades. Extra repayments or money in an offset account save interest at the mortgage rate, which is well below a credit card's. That's why the mortgage usually comes last in an avalanche order.

A typical avalanche order: credit cards, then personal loans and BNPL, then car loans, then HELP and the mortgage. Your own order depends on your actual rates and fees, so it's worth checking each one.

How people choose between them

People who are motivated by numbers often lean towards the avalanche, because it costs less. People who've tried before and lost steam sometimes lean towards the snowball, and see the extra interest as the price of quicker wins. Some mix the two: clear one tiny balance for the boost, then switch to highest rate first.

Some people also keep a small emergency buffer alongside, so the next unexpected bill doesn't go straight back on the card they've been paying down. Others put every spare dollar on the debt first. It's a trade-off between interest saved and having a cushion.

If the debt feels too big

If you're struggling to make minimum payments, or you're using one debt to pay another, you don't have to sort it out alone. The National Debt Helpline (1800 007 007) puts you in touch with free, confidential financial counsellors. They don't lend money or sell anything. They can help you work out a plan, talk to lenders about hardship arrangements, and explain your options.

Things to weigh up

  • How far apart are the interest rates on your debts?
  • Do quick wins help you keep going, or are you happy to wait for the bigger saving?
  • Do any of your loans charge fees for paying extra or paying out early?
  • Is a small cash buffer worth having alongside the extra repayments?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances. For free help with debt specifically, the National Debt Helpline is on 1800 007 007.
