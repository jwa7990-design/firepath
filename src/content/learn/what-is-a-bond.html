The Simple Version: You're the Bank

When most Australians think about investing, they think about shares — buying a small piece of a company and hoping it grows in value. Bonds work differently. When you buy a bond, you're not buying ownership of anything. You're lending money to a government or a company, and they're promising to pay it back — with interest.

Think of it like this: if your neighbour asked to borrow $10,000 and agreed to pay you $400 every year for five years, then return the full $10,000 at the end, that's essentially how a bond works. You're the lender. The borrower pays you regular interest (called a coupon), and at the end of the agreed period (called the maturity date), you get your original money back.

Governments issue bonds to fund things like hospitals, roads, and schools. Companies issue bonds when they want to raise money without selling shares to new investors. Either way, the bond is a formal legal contract — a promise to pay.

How the Interest Works

When a bond is created, it has a face value (typically $1,000 per bond), a fixed interest rate, and an end date. If a bond has a face value of $1,000 and pays 4% per year, you receive $40 annually until the bond matures. Simple enough.

However, bonds are also traded on financial markets, which means their price can go up and down before the maturity date. This is where things get slightly more complex, but it directly affects how bond funds behave.

Key insight: When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall. When interest rates fall, existing bond prices rise. This inverse relationship is one of the most important concepts in fixed income investing.

Here's why: imagine you hold a bond paying 3% interest. Then the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) raises rates and new bonds are now being issued at 5%. Your 3% bond suddenly looks much less attractive, so its market price drops to compensate. Conversely, if rates fall to 1%, your 3% bond looks very appealing and its price rises. This is the fundamental tension in bond investing.

Why Bonds Are Generally Lower Risk Than Shares

Bonds aren't risk-free (no investment is), but they tend to be significantly more stable than shares for a few important reasons.

  • You're a creditor, not an owner: If a company goes under, bondholders get paid before shareholders do. Shareholders often receive nothing in a bankruptcy; bondholders often recover at least some of their money.
  • Predictable income: Unlike share dividends, which companies can cut at any time, bond payments are legally contracted. You know exactly what you're supposed to receive and when.
  • Lower volatility: Bond prices don't usually swing as wildly as share prices. A bond-heavy mix is much less likely to drop 35% in a month the way a share-heavy one can during a crisis.
  • Defensive in downturns: During recessions or sharemarket crashes, investors often move money into bonds (especially government bonds), which can push bond prices up when shares are falling.

Bonds and shares often move in opposite directions, which means holding both can smooth out overall returns. Not always, though. In 2022, rising interest rates pushed both down at the same time.

The trade-off is lower long-term growth. Over long periods, bonds have returned less than shares, so a portfolio with more bonds is steadier but tends to grow more slowly.

Government Bonds vs Corporate Bonds

Not all bonds carry the same level of risk. It's worth understanding the two main types available to Australian investors.

Government Bonds

Australian Government Bonds (also called Commonwealth Government Securities or CGS) are issued by the Australian federal government. These are considered among the safest investments available — the Australian government has never defaulted on its debt. State governments also issue bonds. The trade-off for this safety is a lower interest rate. You won't get rich from government bonds, but you're very unlikely to lose the money you put in.

Corporate Bonds

Corporate bonds are issued by companies like the big banks, Telstra, or Woolworths. Because companies carry more risk of default than governments, they must offer higher interest rates to attract lenders. This extra return is called the credit spread.

Corporate bonds are further divided by credit quality. High-quality companies (called investment grade) offer modest rates with relatively low risk. Lower-quality companies (sometimes called high yield or, less flatteringly, junk bonds) offer higher rates but with meaningfully higher default risk. Many everyday Australian investors stick to investment-grade bonds for that reason, while others accept the extra risk for the higher income.

Worth knowing: ASIC's MoneySmart website has a straightforward explainer on bonds and fixed income that's worth reading if you want to dig deeper into how credit ratings and bond risk are assessed.

How Australian Investors Can Buy Bonds

Buying individual bonds used to be complicated and expensive — largely the domain of institutional investors and the very wealthy. That's changed significantly. Today, most everyday Australian investors access bonds through Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) listed on the ASX.

Bond ETFs work just like share ETFs — you buy units on the ASX through your brokerage account, and the fund holds a diversified basket of bonds on your behalf. You receive regular income distributions (which the ATO treats as interest income, not dividends, so there are no franking credits), and you can buy or sell on any trading day.

Some of the broad categories of bond ETFs available on the ASX include:

  • Australian government bond ETFs — holding Commonwealth and state government bonds
  • Australian composite bond ETFs — mixing government and investment-grade corporate bonds
  • Global bond ETFs — providing exposure to international government and corporate bonds, sometimes currency-hedged back to Australian dollars
  • Short-duration bond ETFs — holding bonds that mature sooner, which are less sensitive to interest rate changes

Fees (the annual management fee, sometimes called the expense ratio) vary across these products and come straight off your returns. Many Australian bond ETFs have management fees ranging from around 0.10% to 0.30% per year.

Bonds in a FIRE Portfolio: Where Do They Fit?

The FIRE movement (Financial Independence, Retire Early) tends to be dominated by growth-focused investors, heavy on shares and light on bonds. Their reasoning is that during the building years there are decades to ride out market falls. Others hold some bonds all the way through, because they sleep better with a steadier balance. Around early retirement, bonds come up more often for a specific reason: sequence of returns risk.

Sequence of returns risk is the danger that a major market crash occurs right at the beginning of your retirement, forcing you to sell shares at low prices to fund living expenses. This can permanently damage a portfolio in a way that a mid-career crash doesn't. Some people hold more bonds at this stage so they can draw on steadier assets during a downturn and give their shares time to recover. Others prefer to stay mostly in shares and accept the swings, or rely on a cash buffer, part-time work or spending less in bad years.

Inside your superannuation fund, you've likely already had some bond exposure — balanced and growth options typically include a blend of shares, property, and fixed income. As you approach your preservation age and retirement, many super funds automatically shift towards more conservative allocations with higher bond weightings. Knowing why this happens makes it easier to compare your fund's options.

One framework some early retirees use is to hold enough bonds (or other defensive assets) outside super to cover two to three years of living expenses. The idea is a buffer that means they don't have to sell shares in a downturn. The cost is that money held this way usually grows more slowly.

Practical consideration: a buffer like this doesn't have to sit entirely in bonds. Some people use a mix of bonds, high-interest savings and cash. What they're after is stability, whatever it's labelled.

Bonds won't make headlines the way shares do. For people who hold them, their job is to soften a bad few years right when the money is needed most. For a look at how a steadier mix typically works in practice, Balanced Funds Explained covers it.

Things to weigh up

  • How many years until you expect to start drawing on your investments?
  • How would you feel, and what would you do, if your balance fell by a third?
  • How much lower growth would you accept in exchange for a steadier ride?
  • What does your super option already hold in bonds and cash?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.
