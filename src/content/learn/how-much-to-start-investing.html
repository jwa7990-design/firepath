A common, quiet belief stops a lot of people from investing: the assumption that you need thousands of dollars before it's "worth it." That assumption is largely outdated.

The myth of needing a large lump sum

Many investment platforms now let you invest fairly small amounts on a regular basis. For many people, a steady habit ends up mattering more than a big deposit. A smaller amount invested regularly for a long period can build more than waiting years to accumulate a "worthy" lump sum, because it has longer to grow.

Common misconception

"I'll start once I have $5,000 saved up." Waiting to accumulate a lump sum often costs more in lost compounding time than it gains in convenience. The years you wait rarely get made up later.

A quick example

Two friends, same age. One waits three years to save a $6,000 lump sum before investing it. The other starts immediately with $40 a week. By year ten, the person who started small and early has generally built a larger balance than the lump-sum waiter — purely because of the extra three years of compounding, even though their total contributions were similar.

Fees at small balances

Some investment products have minimum amounts or fee structures that matter more at very small balances, since a high fixed fee on a tiny balance can eat into returns disproportionately. Some people wait until they have a bit more saved so fees take a smaller bite. Others look for a low-fee option and start small.

Starting small, or waiting

Some people start with whatever they can commit to consistently, because the extra years of growth do a lot of the work. Others prefer to build a cash buffer or clear high-interest debt first, or wait for a lump sum so fees are easier to manage. Both are common.

Things to weigh up

  • Do you have a cash buffer for surprises, and any high-interest debt?
  • What amount could you keep putting in, week after week?
  • How much would fees cost at the amount you're thinking of?
  • How long could you leave the money invested?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.
