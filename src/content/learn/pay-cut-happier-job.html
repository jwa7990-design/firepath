This is a genuinely personal decision with a real financial cost attached — worth calculating rather than either dismissing the number or letting it override everything else.

What actually changes in the calculation

A lower income, if spending stays roughly the same, reduces your savings rate — which extends your projected timeline to financial independence, in rough proportion to how much the income dropped and how much of that was previously going to savings rather than spending.

Common misconception

"A pay cut sets my whole retirement back by years." Often the actual delay is smaller than the size of the pay cut suggests — because only the portion of income that was being saved is what disappears from the calculation.

A quick example

Considering a $15,000 pay cut to leave a draining corporate role for a smaller, more enjoyable business, Tara modelled it properly rather than guessing. Since $9,000 of that would have gone to savings, her projected retirement date moved back roughly two and a half years — a real number, and one she decided was genuinely worth it once it wasn't an abstract fear anymore.

Why the real cost is often smaller than it feels

People often mentally multiply the pay cut by their entire remaining working life, when the actual effect is a shift in the compounding curve — often adding a more modest, specific number of years rather than something catastrophic. Seeing the real number instead of an anxious estimate often makes the trade-off feel more manageable.

What this article can't calculate

Career satisfaction, mental health, and time have real value that doesn't show up in a spreadsheet. The financial modelling here is one input into the decision — a useful one, but not the only one that matters.
