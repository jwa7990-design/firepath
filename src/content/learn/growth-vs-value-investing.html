Two Ways of Looking at the Same Share Market

Walk into any share market and you'll find two broad kinds of companies. Some are growing fast — expanding into new markets, launching products, reinvesting nearly every dollar of profit to get bigger. Others are mature and steady — they've done most of their growing, earn reliable profits, and hand a good chunk of them back to shareholders as dividends. Investors who favour the first kind are called growth investors. Those who favour the second are called value investors.

Neither approach is right or wrong. They're two different answers to the same question: what am I willing to pay today for a company's future profits?

Growth Investing: Paying Up for the Future

Growth companies are expected to increase their earnings much faster than the average business. Think of fast-expanding technology, software or healthcare companies. Because investors expect big profits later, they're willing to pay a high price now — often a price that looks expensive compared with what the company earns today.

Growth companies usually pay small dividends or none at all, because they'd rather reinvest profits into expansion. Your return comes almost entirely from the share price rising as the business grows into its expectations. When those expectations are met, growth investing can be spectacular. When they're not, prices can fall hard and fast.

Value Investing: Buying Good Businesses on Sale

Value investors look for companies trading cheaply compared with their profits, assets or dividends — businesses the market has overlooked, gone off, or just doesn't find exciting. Mature companies such as banks, miners, supermarkets and utilities often fall into this camp.

The bet is that the market is being too pessimistic, and that over time the price will catch up with the company's real worth. In the meantime, value companies often pay meaningful dividends, so part of your return arrives as income along the way.

The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, in plain English: it tells you how many dollars you're paying for each dollar of a company's yearly profit. A company priced at $50 a share that earns $1 per share has a P/E of 50 — you're paying $50 for every $1 of profit. A company at $20 earning $2 per share has a P/E of 10. Growth companies tend to have high P/E ratios because investors expect profits to rise; value companies tend to have low ones.

Common misconception

"Growth shares are risky and value shares are safe." Both carry real risk, just of different kinds. Growth shares can plunge when hoped-for profits don't arrive. Value shares can be cheap for a good reason — a business that's in decline can keep getting cheaper. Investors call that a value trap.

Which One Has Done Better?

The honest answer: it depends entirely on which period you look at. Over long stretches of the twentieth century, academic research found that cheaper "value" shares tended to outperform. Then, for much of the 2010s, growth shares (particularly large global technology companies) left value far behind. Leadership has swung back and forth since.

One thing people take from this: whichever style has just had a great decade can feel obviously right, sometimes just before a quieter spell. Switching styles to chase recent winners risks buying high and selling low.

The Australian Angle

The Australian share market leans heavily towards value-style companies. Our biggest listed businesses are banks and miners — mature, profitable, dividend-paying companies. A fund tracking the ASX 200 therefore tilts towards value and income without you choosing it.

Global share markets, by contrast, are dominated by large technology and growth companies. That's one reason many Australian investors hold both Australian and international shares: together they blend the value tilt at home with the growth tilt abroad.

There's a tax twist too. Many Australian dividend-paying companies attach franking credits to their dividends — credit for tax the company has already paid. That's one reason some local investors like value-style Australian shares, particularly those on lower tax rates or in super's pension phase.

Picking a Side, or Not

Some people building towards financial independence don't pick a side. A broad index fund owns both growth and value companies in proportion to their size, so they automatically hold whichever style is doing well at the time.

Others prefer a fund that deliberately tilts towards one style. Some choose value for the dividend income, or because of the long-run research. Some choose growth because they expect technology and fast-growing companies to keep leading. Tilted funds often cost more and add complexity, and the tilt only helps if that style does better over the years they're held.

Understanding the difference is still valuable. It helps you see what you own, why parts of your portfolio rise and fall at different times, and why a headline about technology shares crashing (or banks rallying) affects your investments the way it does.

Things to weigh up

  • Do you want income along the way, or mostly growth in the share price?
  • How would you feel if your chosen style lagged for ten years?
  • How much extra in fees would a tilted fund cost you each year?
  • How much of your money already leans one way, through Australian or global shares?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.

Growth and value are two halves of the same market. A fund that owns both doesn't have to guess which one wins next.
