The Part of Investing Nobody Warns You About

Most investing guides focus on the numbers: returns, fees, diversification. Far fewer talk about the hardest part — watching your balance fall and not doing anything about it. Volatility is just how much prices move up and down. On a chart it looks like squiggles. In real life it can feel like your stomach dropping every time you open your app.

Learning to handle volatility emotionally is arguably worth more than any stock tip, because the biggest risk to most investors is what they do when the market falls.

Volatility Is Normal — Not a Sign Something Is Broken

Share markets don't rise in a straight line. Falls of 10% or more have historically happened fairly often — roughly every year or two on average. Bigger falls come along less often but are a normal part of investing. During the global financial crisis, Australian shares fell by roughly half between late 2007 and early 2009. In early 2020, they dropped by more than a third in about a month — then recovered most of that fall within the year.

Every one of those falls felt like the end of the world at the time. Every one was eventually followed by recovery and new highs. That's not a guarantee about the future, but it's the pattern long-term investors have lived through again and again.

The key insight: Volatility is the price of admission for long-term growth. The same ups and downs that make shares uncomfortable are the reason they've historically paid more than cash. You can't have one without the other.

Why Falls Feel So Much Worse Than Gains

Our brains aren't built for investing. Research in behavioural finance suggests a loss feels roughly twice as painful as an equal gain feels good — so a $10,000 fall hurts far more than a $10,000 rise delights. Add a few other very human tendencies and you can see why smart people make poor decisions in a downturn:

  • Recency bias: assuming whatever just happened will keep happening. After a fall, it feels like prices will only go lower.
  • Herd behaviour: when headlines scream and everyone you know is selling, doing nothing feels reckless.
  • The urge to act: in most of life, doing something is better than doing nothing. In a market fall, it's often the opposite.
Common misconception

"When markets fall, I should get out and buy back in once things settle." The trouble is that nobody rings a bell at the bottom. Some of the market's best days have historically arrived very close to its worst ones, often while the news was still grim. Investors who sell to wait it out frequently miss the rebound — and lock in the very loss they were trying to avoid.

Practical Ways to Ride Out the Bumps

Keep an emergency fund

The most common reason people sell at the bottom is needing the money, even more than panic. A cash buffer of a few months' expenses means a job loss or big bill never forces you to sell investments while prices are down.

Automate your investing

Investing a set amount on a regular schedule (known as dollar cost averaging) takes emotion out of the decision. When prices fall, your regular contribution automatically buys more units. Your super already works this way: every pay cycle, your contributions buy in, whatever the market is doing.

Look less often

The more frequently you check, the more often you'll see losses. Over a single day, markets fall almost as often as they rise. Over decades, they've risen far more often. Checking quarterly instead of daily is just matching your attention to your timeframe.

Write down your plan before you need it

Decide in calm times what you'll do in rough ones: "If markets fall 30%, I'll keep my contributions going and won't sell." Reading your own words in a downturn is surprisingly steadying.

Worked example Imagine your portfolio is worth $120,000 and markets fall 20%. Your app now shows $96,000 — a $24,000 drop that feels awful. But if you keep investing $500 a fortnight, each contribution now buys about 25% more units than it did before the fall. When prices recover, those cheaper units recover too. The fall feels like a loss in the moment; for someone still building wealth, it's quietly a sale.

Volatility Close to Freedom

Volatility matters most just before and just after you stop working, because a big fall early in retirement, when you're drawing money out, can do lasting damage. This is called sequence of returns risk. Many people approaching financial independence hold a larger cash buffer, perhaps a year or two of expenses, so they can avoid selling shares during a downturn and let the market recover first.

You can't control the market's mood swings. You can control whether they make your decisions for you.
