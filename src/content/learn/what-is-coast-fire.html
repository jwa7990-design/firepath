"You need to keep saving for retirement right up until the day you stop working." That's the assumption almost everyone carries, and for some people it's already not true. Of all the FIRE concepts, Coast FIRE is the one that surprises people most often.

What Coast FIRE means

Coast FIRE is the point where your current invested savings, left completely untouched with zero further contributions, will compound on their own to your full retirement number by a normal retirement age. Once you're there, your existing money is projected to "coast" to the finish line through investment growth alone, if returns hold up. Some people at this point keep saving anyway for a safety margin or an earlier date. Others ease off and spend more, work less, or put money towards other goals.

Common misconception

"Coast FIRE means I've already retired." It doesn't. It means your retirement savings are projected to be on track. You'd likely still need income to cover today's living costs. What changes is that income may no longer need to also fund retirement.

A quick example

Josh, 33, has $260,000 invested and wants to retire at 60 with a $1.6 million freedom number (enough to spend about $64,000 a year). Projected at 7% a year after inflation with no further contributions, $260,000 grows to about $1.6 million by 60. On those assumptions, Josh has already hit Coast FIRE, a decade earlier than he expected. A few weaker years of returns could push that out, which is why some people in his spot keep a buffer.

How to check

Take your current investable balance (outside super, since that's a separate locked timeline), and project it forward at a reasonable long-term return — commonly 7% — to your target retirement age. If that projection alone reaches roughly 25 times your planned retirement spending, you've reached Coast FIRE. If you're specifically wondering whether this means you could reduce hours now, Can I Afford to Work Less? takes that next step.

Why it's often closer than people think

Compounding does most of the work over long timeframes. Someone in their early-to-mid 30s who started investing consistently in their 20s may already be far closer to this threshold than they'd assume, just because a decade or more of growth is already baked into their existing balance.

You might already be coasting towards freedom without realising it. Your own numbers are the way to find out.
