"On track" gets used constantly and defined rarely. Most people who ask this question have never run the numbers — they're going on a feeling, usually driven by anxiety rather than evidence.

Defining "on track" properly

Being on track means: at your current savings rate, invested at a reasonable long-term return, you'll reach a portfolio of roughly 25 times your planned retirement spending by the age you want to stop working. That's it — three inputs (savings rate, current balance, target spending) and one comparison (does the maths land on your desired timeline).

Common misconception

"If I'm saving something every month, I must be on track." Saving *something* and saving *enough for your specific timeline* are different things. The only way to know which one is true is to run the projection.

A quick example

Sarah is 42. She has $120,000 invested and saves $2,800 a month. She wants $70,000 a year in retirement, which puts her freedom number at $1.75 million. Projected forward at a 7% return, her current savings rate gets her there at 61 — five years later than the 56 she was hoping for. Now she knows the real gap, instead of a vague sense that "it'll probably be fine."

The three-question self-check

Three questions do most of the work: What do I spend per year? What's my current savings rate? What age do I want to stop working? Run those three answers through the 25x-spending formula and a compounding projection, and you get a real year to aim for instead of a feeling. If your number already looks strong, What Is Coast FIRE explains how some people think about being further along than they expected.

If the answer is "not yet"

Discovering you're not on track for your ideal timeline isn't a failure. It's useful information. The main things that move the number are your savings rate, your planned spending and the age you're aiming for. Which of those you'd change, if any, is up to you. Because of compounding, even a modest change sustained over years can shift the outcome more than people expect.

"On track" is something you can work out. Once you do, the worry usually shrinks to fit the real gap.
