Investing can feel like a closed club with its own language. The starting point is simpler than the jargon makes it seem.

Before investing anything

Many people set aside a small emergency buffer first — enough to cover an unexpected cost without needing to sell investments at a bad time. Beyond that, starting small is fine.

Common misconception

"I need to understand the stock market properly before I start." Plenty of people start without picking shares or timing anything. A broad index fund, for example, spreads your money across many companies for you. Understanding tends to come with time.

A quick example

Chloe, 24, had never invested and assumed she needed thousands and deep market knowledge to begin. She started with $50 a week into a broad diversified fund, without picking individual companies or trying to time anything. Eighteen months later, she'd built a habit, a small but real nest egg and, more importantly, the confidence that investing wasn't as complicated as she'd imagined.

The simplest starting concept

An index fund (often bought as an ETF) is a single investment that owns a small slice of hundreds of companies at once. It spreads your money across a whole market instead of betting on one company. Other starting points people use include their super fund's investment options, a diversified managed fund, or a few individual shares for those who enjoy the research.

How people start, practically

Most beginner investors start with small, regular contributions (weekly or monthly) into a broad, diversified fund, rather than trying to pick winning individual shares. This regular approach (sometimes called dollar-cost averaging) smooths out the effect of price swings over time, since you buy at many different prices rather than trying to time a single perfect moment.
