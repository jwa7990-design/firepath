An emergency fund is money set aside for the stuff nobody plans for. The car needs a new gearbox. The vet bill lands. Your hours get cut. Without a buffer, those moments usually end up on a credit card at 20% interest, and that's how one bad month turns into a year of catching up.

So how much is enough? There's no single right figure, but there is a sensible way to work out yours.

Why three months is the usual starting point

Most guides, including the government's Moneysmart site, suggest a target of about three months of expenses. That's long enough to cover a big one-off bill, or a few weeks between jobs, without being so large it takes forever to build.

Three months is a starting point, though. Some people need more, and the reasons are pretty predictable. Six months tends to make more sense if:

  1. Your household runs on one income. If that pay stops, everything stops. Two incomes give you a natural backup.
  2. Your work is contract, casual or seasonal. Gaps between contracts are normal, and there's often no redundancy pay or notice period to soften them.
  3. You've got kids. Costs are higher and harder to cut quickly. School fees and childcare don't pause because you're between jobs.
  4. You've got a mortgage. Rent you can sometimes reduce by moving. Mortgage repayments are fixed, and falling behind gets expensive fast.

On the other hand, someone on a secure salary, with a partner who also works and no dependants, might feel fine at three months or a bit less.

Spending, not income

The target is based on what you spend, not what you earn. If you take home $5,500 a month but spend $3,800, it's the $3,800 that needs covering. In a real emergency you'd also trim the extras, like takeaway and subscriptions, so your "bare bones" number might be lower again.

Common misconception

"I need three months of my salary sitting in the bank." That's a much bigger target than most people need, and it can make the goal feel out of reach. The usual measure is spending, and a month of tracking will show what yours is.

A worked example

Worked example Jess, 28, is a retail manager in Newcastle. She rents with a flatmate and spends about $3,800 a month. Three months of spending is $11,400. Six months would be $22,800.

Her job's steady and she has no kids, so she starts with the three-month target. Putting aside $200 a week, she'd get there in about 57 weeks. At $300 a week, it's about 38 weeks. If she later moves to contract work, she could keep building towards six months.

Where people keep it

An emergency fund has two jobs: be there when you need it, and not lose value while it waits. That rules out shares, which can drop 20% in the same month you lose your job. Most Australians keep it in one of two places.

A high-interest savings account

This is a savings account separate from your everyday account, usually paying a higher rate if you meet certain conditions, like depositing a set amount each month or not withdrawing. Rates move with the RBA cash rate and have recently been around 4–5% for the better accounts.

The trade-off: the interest is taxed as income at your marginal rate. Jess earns enough to be in the 30% tax bracket, so with the 2% Medicare levy she keeps 68 cents of each dollar of interest. On $11,400 at 4.5%, that's about $513 of interest, or roughly $349 after tax. Money in the bank is protected by the government's Financial Claims Scheme up to $250,000 per person per bank.

A mortgage offset account

If you have a home loan, an offset account is a transaction account linked to it. Your balance is subtracted from the loan before interest is worked out, usually daily. You don't earn interest. You save it instead, at your mortgage rate, and that saving isn't taxed because it isn't income.

So $11,400 in an offset against a 6% loan saves about $684 a year in interest, with no tax to pay. That's often more than a savings account would leave you after tax. The catch is mostly behavioural. Your emergency money sits next to your spending money, and it can quietly drift into "general funds". Some people keep a mental floor on the balance, or use a second offset account labelled for emergencies.

The core trade-off: a savings account keeps the money separate and earns taxed interest. An offset saves you interest at your mortgage rate, tax-free, but sits closer to everyday spending. Either works. What matters is that the money's there and you can get to it within a day or two.

A redraw facility (pulling back extra repayments you've made on your loan) is sometimes used too. It works much like an offset for saving interest, but lenders can limit or change redraw access, so it's a less certain place for money you might need in a hurry.

Building it while you're paying off debt

If you've got credit card debt, it can feel wrong to save at 4.5% while you're paying 20% interest. On the maths alone, the card wins. But with no buffer at all, the next surprise bill goes straight back on the card, and you're back where you started.

A common approach is to do it in stages:

  1. A small starter buffer first, say $1,000–$2,000, so a minor emergency doesn't go on the card.
  2. Then extra money towards high-interest debt, like credit cards and personal loans, with minimum payments kept up on everything else.
  3. Once that debt's gone, the full fund, built from the money that used to go on repayments.

Which order suits you depends on how secure your income is and how much you're paying in interest. If your debts are getting on top of you, the National Debt Helpline (1800 007 007) offers free, confidential help from financial counsellors.

When is it "enough"?

You've got enough when a surprise bill or a few months without pay wouldn't force you into new debt or into selling investments at a bad time. For most people that's somewhere between three and six months of spending.

Past that point, extra cash sitting in savings tends to lose ground to inflation after tax. That's usually when people start thinking about what comes next: paying down the mortgage faster, adding to super, or investing for the long term.

It's also worth revisiting the number when life changes. A new baby, a mortgage, a move to contract work or a partner stopping work can all shift what "enough" looks like. And if the fund gets used, many people make topping it back up their first priority.

An emergency fund won't make you rich. What it does is stop a bad month from undoing the progress you've made everywhere else.
