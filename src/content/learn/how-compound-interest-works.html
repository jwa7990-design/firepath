Why Compounding Matters So Much

You've probably heard compound interest called the "eighth wonder of the world". That's a stretch, but not by much. It's one of the most important ideas for any Australian building long-term wealth, yet most of us were never properly taught how it works.

Once you see it with real numbers, it's hard to ignore. And the earlier you grasp it, the more it works in your favour.

Simple Interest vs Compound Interest: The Key Difference

Before we get to the numbers, it helps to understand the distinction between simple interest and compound interest.

With simple interest, you earn a return only on your original amount. If you invest $10,000 at 7% simple interest, you earn $700 every year — no more, no less. After 10 years, you have $17,000.

With compound interest, you earn a return on your original amount plus all the interest you've already earned. Your interest earns interest. That difference, over time, is enormous.

The core idea: Compound interest means your money grows on top of its own previous growth. Each year's gains become the starting point for the next year's gains.

The Real Numbers: $10,000 at 7% Over Time

Let's use a straightforward example. You invest $10,000 as a lump sum and achieve a 7% average annual return — a historically reasonable long-run figure for a diversified Australian share portfolio, roughly in line with the long-term average of the ASX 200 including dividends (though past performance never guarantees future results).

Here's what compound growth looks like over different timeframes:

  • After 10 years: $19,672 — your money has nearly doubled
  • After 20 years: $38,697 — almost four times your original investment
  • After 30 years: $76,123 — more than seven and a half times your starting amount

Notice the pattern. The first 10 years added roughly $9,700. The second 10 years added about $19,000. The third 10 years added nearly $37,500. The same 10-year period produces more than three times the dollar growth at the end than it did at the beginning. That's compounding at work — the longer it runs, the faster it grows.

Compare this to simple interest at the same rate: after 30 years you'd have just $31,000. Compounding delivers more than twice that result from the same starting point and same rate.

Why Starting Early Matters More Than the Amount You Invest

This is where compounding gets surprising. Consider two Australians: Priya and Jordan.

  • Priya starts investing at age 25. She puts in $10,000 and never adds another dollar. By age 65 (40 years at 7%), she has approximately $149,745.
  • Jordan waits until age 35 to start. He also invests $10,000 and never adds another dollar. By age 65 (30 years at 7%), he has approximately $76,123.

Priya ends up with nearly twice as much money, despite investing the exact same amount. The only difference is that she started 10 years earlier. That decade of early growth compounds relentlessly for the remaining 30 years.

Time in the market matters more than the size of your first investment. A smaller amount invested early will often outperform a larger amount invested late. This is why the timing of a start matters so much: each year of delay is a year of compounding that can't be got back.

This principle applies directly to superannuation. Your compulsory super contributions (12% of your salary under Australia's Superannuation Guarantee) are quietly compounding inside your fund for decades. The decisions you make in your 20s and 30s about which fund to choose, and whether to make voluntary contributions, can easily be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars by retirement. ASIC's MoneySmart calculator is a free tool that lets you model this for your own situation.

The Rule of 72: A Simple Mental Shortcut

You don't need a spreadsheet to estimate how long it takes your money to double. The Rule of 72 is a quick mental calculation used by investors and financial professionals around the world.

Divide 72 by your annual interest rate, and the result is approximately how many years it takes to double your money.

  • At 7% return: 72 ÷ 7 = about 10.3 years to double
  • At 4% return: 72 ÷ 4 = 18 years to double
  • At 2% return: 72 ÷ 2 = 36 years to double
  • At 10% return: 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2 years to double

The Rule of 72 also works in reverse — it shows you how quickly inflation erodes your purchasing power. If inflation runs at 3% (close to the RBA's target band of 2–3%), your money's purchasing power halves in roughly 24 years. It's one of the main reasons that holding cash long-term is a risk of its own.

Saving in a Bank vs Investing: The Real-World Gap

Most Australians keep the bulk of their savings in a bank account. It feels safe, and it is — deposits up to $250,000 per institution are protected under the Australian Government's Financial Claims Scheme. But safety comes at a cost.

Even the best high-interest savings accounts in Australia usually pay somewhere around 4–5%. That sounds reasonable, but consider two important factors:

Interest is taxed as income

The ATO treats interest earned in a savings account as ordinary income. If you're on a marginal tax rate of 30% (which applies to income between $45,001 and $135,000), a 5% savings rate becomes roughly 3.5% after tax. At 45%, it drops closer to 2.75%.

Inflation eats into what's left

Once you subtract inflation from that already-taxed return, your return after tax and inflation on a savings account can be very close to zero — or even negative in periods of higher inflation.

A long-term diversified investment in Australian and international shares, by contrast, has historically delivered average returns after inflation well above cash. Returns from shares are also often more tax-efficient for Australian investors, thanks to franking credits on Australian dividends — a system where companies pass on tax they've already paid to shareholders, reducing the investor's own tax bill.

Savings accounts have their place. They're the usual home for emergency funds and short-term goals, and some people prefer their certainty for longer-term money too. For money left alone for 10 or more years, the compounding gap between a savings account and a diversified investment portfolio can add up to hundreds of thousands of dollars over a working life, though the investing side comes with more ups and downs.

A savings account suits money you need soon. The cost creeps in when you treat "safe" and "growing" as the same thing. If you want to see this same logic applied to your own numbers rather than a generic example, Compounding With Your Actual Numbers walks through it.
