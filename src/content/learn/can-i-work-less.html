Here's something worth knowing: some people asking this have already reached the point where the answer is yes, and don't know it. This question usually shows up during burnout rather than careful planning. That's why a proper calculation can help more than an anxious guess either way.

The idea that answers this: Coast FIRE

If your current invested savings, left completely alone with no further contributions, would compound to your full retirement number by a normal retirement age purely through investment growth, you've reached what's called Coast FIRE. At that point, you technically don't need to keep saving for retirement specifically. That frees up income for reduced hours, a career change, or just some breathing room. The full mechanics are in What Is Coast FIRE?

Common misconception

"Working less means giving up on retirement." Not necessarily. If you've already reached Coast FIRE, then on those assumptions reducing hours doesn't change your projected retirement — it only changes your income and lifestyle right now, which is a separate, more immediate calculation.

A quick example

Marcus, 39, has $365,000 invested and a $1.4 million freedom number at his planned retirement age of 60. Projected at 7% with zero further contributions, his current balance alone reaches that target by 59 — meaning he's essentially already Coast FIRE. Dropping to four days a week wouldn't change his eventual retirement date at all; it would only change what the next twenty years feel like.

How to model it

Take your current invested balance (excluding super, since that's a separate locked timeline), and project it forward at a reasonable long-term return to your target retirement age. If it already reaches your 25x-spending number without another dollar added, working less doesn't threaten your eventual retirement — it just changes your income and lifestyle right now, which is a completely separate, more immediate calculation (can your reduced income cover your reduced-hours cost of living).

What if you haven't reached it yet?

Working less is still possible — it just means your retirement timeline moves later, in direct proportion to how much less you're contributing. Some people decide that trade is worth it for more time now. Others would rather keep their timeline. Seeing it in real years, rather than guessing, can make the choice clearer.

Things to weigh up

  • Would your reduced income still cover your day-to-day costs, with a buffer?
  • How many years later would your projected date move, and how do you feel about that?
  • What would less super going in mean for your later years?
  • How sure are you about the return you've assumed?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.

The useful question is what working less would cost you in years, and whether you're happy with that trade.
