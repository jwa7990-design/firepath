The Age Pension is the government's fortnightly payment for older Australians. Plenty of people assume it's only for those with nothing saved. In fact a lot of retirees with a decent super balance and a paid-off home still get a part pension. Here's how it works, using the rates from 20 September 2026.

Who can get it

You need to be 67 or older and an Australian resident. Generally you need at least 10 years of Australian residence, with at least 5 of those in one unbroken stretch (there are some exceptions, for example under international agreements). Then two means tests decide how much you get.

How much it pays

From 20 September 2026, the maximum rates, including the pension and energy supplements, are:

Rates go up twice a year, in March and September, in line with prices and wages.

The assets test

Centrelink adds up what you own, then compares it against limits. Your family home isn't counted, but most other things are: super (once you're 67, or once it's paying you a pension), shares, bank accounts, investment property, cars, caravans and household contents (at what they'd sell for, not what you paid).

You get the full pension up to a threshold. Above it, your pension drops by $3 a fortnight for every $1,000 over. That's $78 a year per $1,000.

Non-homeowners get a higher limit to allow for the fact their wealth isn't tied up in a house.

The income test and deeming

The income test looks at what you earn: wages, rent from an investment property, and income from your financial assets. You can have up to $226 a fortnight (single) or $396 a fortnight (couple, combined) before it bites. Above that, your pension drops by 50c for each extra dollar.

For financial assets like super in pension phase, shares, ETFs and bank savings, Centrelink doesn't look at what you earn or withdraw. It uses deeming: it assumes your money earns a set rate. From 20 September 2026:

  • 1.75% on the first $66,800 (single) or $110,600 (couple, combined);
  • 3.75% on anything above that.

So if your investments do better than the deeming rate, the extra isn't counted. And drawing money out of your super doesn't count as income in itself.

You're assessed under both tests, and whichever gives the lower pension is the one that applies. For most homeowners with a decent nest egg, that's the assets test.

Common misconception

"Having super means I won't get the Age Pension." A single homeowner can have up to $745,750 in assessable assets (and a couple $1,121,000) and still get something. As your balance runs down over retirement, the pension usually goes up.

When only one of you is 67

Couples are assessed together, using the couple limits on your combined assets and income. If one partner is 67 and the other is younger, only the older partner can get the Age Pension, at up to the half-couple rate ($933 a fortnight).

The helpful part: the younger partner's super is not counted while it's still in accumulation phase (building up, not paying a pension) and they're under Age Pension age. That can make a real difference for couples with an age gap. Once the younger partner turns 67, or starts a pension from that super, it's counted.

Worked example

Graham and Lorraine are both 67 and own their home in Ballarat. Between them they have $640,000 of super paying them a pension, $50,000 in the bank and $60,000 worth of cars and household contents. That's $750,000 of assessable assets, $690,000 of it financial.

Assets test: they're $251,000 over the couple homeowner threshold of $499,000. At $3 a fortnight per $1,000, that's $753 a fortnight off the $1,866 maximum, leaving $1,113 a fortnight.

Income test: deeming gives $1,935 on the first $110,600 plus $21,728 on the rest: about $23,660 a year, or $910 a fortnight. That's $514 over their free area, which takes $257 a fortnight off, leaving $1,609.

The assets test gives the lower figure, so it applies. They'd get about $1,113 a fortnight combined, or roughly $28,940 a year. If they also draw $40,000 a year from their super, their total income is close to $69,000, and the pension goes up as their balance falls.

How the Age Pension fits a FIRE plan

If you're aiming to stop work early, the Age Pension isn't something to live on in your 40s or 50s. But it can top up your plan later. Many early retirees spend their own savings fairly steadily through their 50s and 60s. By 67, a smaller balance can mean a part pension, and that can take pressure off what you need to draw each year from then on.

It's worth keeping in mind that it's means-tested and the rules can change, so it's sensible to treat it as a possible top-up rather than something guaranteed. Planning tools that model it (like the Freedom gap) can show you how much it might add on your numbers.
