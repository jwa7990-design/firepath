A big part of this question is the interest rate on your debt, compared with what investing might return instead. It isn't the only part. Your cash buffer, your timeframe and how you feel about owing money matter too.

How each option works

Every dollar you pay off a debt saves that debt's rate of interest, guaranteed. Investing has a long-run return commonly estimated at around 7% a year after inflation for diversified shares, but it isn't guaranteed and can be negative in any given year. A 20% card rate is higher than that assumed return. A 4% mortgage rate is lower. The closer the two numbers are, the more the other factors come into it.

Common misconception

"All debt should be paid off before investing." Not all debt is equal. A 20% credit card and a 4% mortgage work out very differently on the maths — lumping them together as "debt" hides the decision that matters.

A quick example

Ryan has $8,000 on a credit card at 19% interest, and a mortgage at 5.8%. Paying down the credit card saves him 19% interest, guaranteed. That's well above the 7% long-run return commonly assumed for shares. His mortgage, at 5.8%, sits closer to expected long-term investment returns. Some people in Ryan's spot invest alongside a mortgage like that. Others prefer to pay it down faster.

Where it gets more nuanced

Lower-interest debt, like a mortgage or HECS/HELP (which is indexed rather than charged commercial interest), sits in a different category. Some people prefer to invest alongside these debts, because the saving from paying them off early is lower than the growth they expect from investing over time. Others prefer to pay them down faster, because that saving is certain and investment returns aren't.

It isn't only about the maths

Some people place real value on being debt-free: peace of mind, lower repayments and fewer things that can go wrong. Others value having money invested and building, with the chance of higher growth and a bit more flexibility. Both are reasonable choices. What matters is that it fits your situation and you've made it knowingly.

Things to weigh up

  • What interest rate is each of your debts charging?
  • How much cash would you want on hand for emergencies alongside either option?
  • How would you feel if markets fell while you still had debt?
  • Does any of your debt work differently, like HECS indexation or a tax-deductible investment loan?
  • How much does being debt-free matter to you?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.

The interest rate is a good place to start. Where you go from there is up to you.
