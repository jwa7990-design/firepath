One number decides this whole question: the interest rate on your debt. Your personality and whether you're "a saver or a spender" don't come into it. What matters is that one figure, compared against what investing would realistically return instead.

The decision rule

If your debt's interest rate is higher than the return you'd reasonably expect from investing (commonly estimated at around 7% a year after inflation for diversified shares over the long term), paying off that debt first is the stronger move on the maths. Every dollar you pay off saves that rate of interest, guaranteed. If the debt's rate is lower than that, investing alongside it can make sense.

Common misconception

"All debt should be paid off before investing." Not all debt is equal. A 20% credit card and a 4% mortgage deserve completely different treatment — lumping them together as "debt" hides the decision that matters.

A quick example

Ryan has $8,000 on a credit card at 19% interest, and a mortgage at 5.8%. Paying down the credit card first saves him 19% interest, guaranteed. No realistic investment beats that. His mortgage, at 5.8%, sits closer to expected long-term investment returns, making "invest alongside it" a reasonable choice rather than a mistake.

Where it gets more nuanced

Lower-interest debt, like a mortgage or HECS/HELP (which is indexed rather than charged commercial interest), sits in a different category. Many people reasonably choose to invest alongside these debts rather than aggressively paying them off early, since the "return" from eliminating them is lower than realistic investment growth over time.

The exception worth naming

The maths isn't the only thing that counts. Some people place real value on being debt-free for peace of mind, even where the numbers would favour investing instead. That's a legitimate personal choice, not a financial error, as long as it's made knowingly rather than by default.

The rule of thumb: pay off high-interest debt first. Low-interest debt is more of a judgement call.
