Search this question and you'll get a dozen different answers: $500,000. $1 million. $2 million if you want to be "comfortable." What the maths says is that none of these numbers mean anything without knowing one thing first — how much you actually spend.

The formula behind every real answer

Most credible retirement calculators, including FirePath, use a version of the same formula: your freedom number is roughly 25 times your annual spending. This comes from the "4% rule" — historical research suggesting you can withdraw about 4% of a well-spread mix of investments each year, adjusted for inflation, with a strong likelihood of it lasting 30+ years.

So if you spend $50,000 a year, your number is around $1.25 million. If you spend $80,000, it's $2 million. The oft-quoted "$1 million to retire" only applies to someone spending about $40,000 a year — it was never a universal figure.

Common misconception

"I need $2 million to retire." Not necessarily. Someone spending $55,000 a year has a very different target from someone spending $140,000 a year — the headline figure you see quoted usually assumes a specific spending level that may have nothing to do with yours.

A quick example

Imagine Priya, 38. She spends $62,000 a year and expects similar spending in retirement. Her freedom number is $62,000 × 25 = $1.55 million — not the $2 million she'd assumed from a headline she'd read, and not the $1 million a colleague on a leaner budget was aiming for either. Once she ran her own number, the target stopped feeling like a moving goalpost.

Why income-based answers mislead people

Two people earning $100,000 a year can need completely different amounts. One spends $90,000 and needs a huge portfolio. The other spends $50,000 and needs a much smaller one. Income tells you how fast you can save — spending tells you how big the target is.

The Australian layer: super changes the maths

For most Australians, part of this number is already being built for you — inside superannuation. Since you generally can't get to your super until your preservation age (60 for most people), your real calculation often splits into two: how much you need accessible before 60, and how much can come from super after that. If you're not sure whether that gap applies to you, What Happens to Your Money Before You Can Access Super? walks through it.

Skip the headline figures. Your own number is easier to work out than the internet makes it look.
