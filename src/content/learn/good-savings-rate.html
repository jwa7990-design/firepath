Financial independence communities often throw around numbers like "50% savings rate" as if it's a baseline expectation. What most people overlook is that at most incomes, that's not realistic, and treating it as the benchmark does more harm than good.

Why savings rate matters more than income

Savings rate (the percentage of your income you save and invest) is the single biggest factor in how long you'll need to work. It does two jobs at once: it builds your investments, and because higher savings usually means lower spending, it also shrinks the target you're saving towards.

Common misconception

"A good savings rate is whatever the FIRE forums say." Forums are dominated by high-income, no-dependant households. A "good" rate for a nurse supporting two kids on a single income looks completely different, and is just as good.

A quick example

Two households, both saving $500 a month. One earns $60,000 (a savings rate around 12%) — a strong starting point given their costs. The other earns $180,000 (a savings rate around 4%) — meaning there's likely real room to increase it without much lifestyle change. Same dollar figure, very different story.

What's realistic at different income levels

At lower incomes, where a larger share goes to essentials, even 5-10% is a strong starting point, and building the habit matters more than the dollar amount. At middle incomes, 15-25% is a solid, sustainable target for most households. At higher incomes, 30%+ becomes realistic once lifestyle costs don't scale up at the same rate as pay. If you're unsure which category you're in, Am I Bad With Money or Just Underpaid? can help separate the two.

The trap to watch for

Lifestyle inflation, where spending quietly rises every time income does, is the main reason savings rates stall even as people earn more. The most effective habit is saving a fixed percentage of every future pay rise before it becomes a fixed cost.

The best savings rate is one you can keep up for twenty years without resenting it.
