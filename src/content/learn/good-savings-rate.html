Financial independence communities often throw around numbers like "50% savings rate" as if it's a baseline expectation. What often gets overlooked is that at most incomes, that's not realistic, and treating it as the benchmark can leave people feeling they've failed before they start.

Why savings rate matters so much

Savings rate (the percentage of your income you save and invest) is one of the biggest factors in how long you'd need to work, often more than income itself. It does two jobs at once: it builds your investments, and because higher savings usually means lower spending, it also shrinks the target you're saving towards.

Common misconception

"A good savings rate is whatever the FIRE forums say." Forums are dominated by high-income, no-dependant households. A "good" rate for a nurse supporting two kids on a single income looks completely different, and is just as good.

A quick example

Two households, both saving $500 a month. One earns $60,000 (a savings rate around 12%) — a strong starting point given their costs. The other earns $180,000 (a savings rate under 5%) — so there may be room to increase it without much lifestyle change. Same dollar figure, very different story.

What's realistic at different income levels

At lower incomes, where a larger share goes to essentials, many people see 5-10% as a strong start, and some find building the habit matters more than the dollar amount. At middle incomes, 15-25% is a range many households find they can keep up. At higher incomes, 30%+ can become realistic once lifestyle costs don't scale up at the same rate as pay. If you're unsure which category you're in, Am I Bad With Money or Just Underpaid? can help separate the two.

A common trap

Lifestyle inflation, where spending quietly rises every time income does, is a common reason savings rates stall even as people earn more. A habit many people find works is saving a fixed percentage of each pay rise before it becomes a fixed cost.

Things to weigh up

  • How much of your income goes on essentials you can't easily trim?
  • What rate could you keep up for years, not just a few months?
  • How much does spending now matter to you, compared with stopping work sooner?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.

For a lot of people, a good savings rate is one they can keep up for twenty years without resenting it.
