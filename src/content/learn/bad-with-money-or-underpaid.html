This question matters because the fix is completely different depending on the answer — and a lot of people spend years applying the wrong one.

A practical way to tell the difference

If your essential living costs (housing, food, transport, utilities) already consume most or all of your income, no amount of budgeting discipline can create savings that don't exist — that's an income problem, not a spending problem. If, on the other hand, there's real discretionary spending happening each month but nothing is left over afterwards, that's closer to a spending problem, or at least a case for closer tracking.

Common misconception

"I just need to budget harder." If essentials already consume your whole income, a tighter budget on discretionary spending that doesn't really exist won't create room — there's nothing in the maths left to squeeze.

A quick example

Jade earns $52,000 and spends $49,000 on rent, food, transport, and bills — almost nothing discretionary. No amount of "cutting back on coffee" changes that picture; the gap is structural. Meanwhile her friend Owen earns $95,000, spends $91,000, and has $15,000 a year in discretionary spending he's never tracked. Same "no savings left" outcome, with a completely different cause underneath.

Why this distinction matters

Treating an income problem as a spending problem often just adds guilt on top of a structural issue — cutting a $6 coffee doesn't meaningfully change things if rent alone takes half your pay. Conversely, treating a real spending problem as an income problem can mean missing real, available room to build savings. If your income looks adequate on paper but the numbers never work out, What's a Good Savings Rate for My Income? is a useful next check.

What each answer points towards

If it's an income problem, what works best long-term is usually skills, negotiation, or a role change, rather than smaller daily sacrifices. If it's a spending problem, tracking where money actually goes for a month (often more revealing than people expect) tends to surface the real gap.

Most people are a bit of both. Knowing which way you lean tells you what to work on first.
