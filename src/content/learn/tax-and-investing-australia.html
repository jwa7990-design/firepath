Why tax matters for investors

Many Australians invest to build wealth over the long term, but the returns you see on paper aren't always the returns you keep. Tax is one of the biggest real costs of investing, yet it's one that many Australian investors don't fully understand until they receive a surprise bill from the Australian Taxation Office (ATO). A basic grasp of how investment income is taxed can make a real difference to what you keep over time.

This article covers the core tax concepts that apply to most Australian investors. It's general information, not personal tax advice. For guidance on your own situation, a registered tax agent or licensed financial adviser can help.

Capital Gains Tax (CGT) — selling investments

When you sell an investment for more than you paid for it, the profit is called a capital gain. In Australia, capital gains are taxed as part of your ordinary income — they're added to your taxable income for that financial year and taxed at your marginal rate.

How CGT works in practice

Say you bought shares on the ASX for $5,000 and sold them later for $8,000. Your capital gain is $3,000. That $3,000 gets added to your other income — salary, freelance work, interest, dividends — and you pay tax on the total at your marginal rate. If you're in the 30% tax bracket, you'd owe around $900 in tax on that gain.

The 12-month CGT discount

Here's one of the most valuable rules in Australian tax law for investors: individuals and trusts that hold an asset for at least 12 months can generally get a 50% CGT discount. The ATO sets out who qualifies. Using the same example, your $3,000 gain would be halved to $1,500 before being added to your income — cutting your tax bill roughly in half.

Key insight: Holding an investment for at least 12 months before selling can halve the tax you pay on your profit. This is one reason long-term investing can be more tax-efficient than frequent trading.

When do you pay CGT?

CGT is triggered when you dispose of an asset — that means selling shares, selling an investment property, or transferring ownership. Just holding an investment that has gone up in value doesn't trigger a tax event. You pay CGT when you lodge your annual tax return for the financial year in which the sale occurred.

How dividends are taxed

When Australian companies make a profit, they often distribute a portion to shareholders as dividends. These are a form of income, and like most income, they're taxable.

Dividends are added to your assessable income and taxed at your marginal rate. However, Australia has a system that prevents company profits from being taxed twice — and it's one of the most important concepts for local share investors to understand.

Franking credits explained simply

Australian companies pay corporate tax (currently 30% for large companies, 25% for smaller ones) on their profits before distributing dividends. The franking credit system (also called imputation credits) recognises that tax has already been paid and gives shareholders a credit for it.

When a company pays a fully franked dividend, it means the company has already paid 30% tax on those earnings. You receive the cash dividend plus a franking credit representing the tax already paid. When you lodge your tax return, you include both the dividend and the franking credit as income — but the credit is then used to offset what you owe.

  • If your marginal tax rate is higher than 30%, you pay the difference.
  • If your marginal tax rate is lower than 30%, you may receive a tax refund for the excess credit.
  • If your marginal rate is exactly 30%, the credit wipes out your tax liability on that dividend entirely.

Key insight: Franking credits are particularly valuable for retirees and low-income earners, who can receive refunds for credits that exceed their tax liability. For high-income earners, they still reduce the tax on dividends, though they don't wipe it out.

Tax inside superannuation

Superannuation is Australia's retirement savings system, and its tax treatment is the main reason it's often called one of the most tax-effective ways for Australians to invest.

The 15% contributions tax

When your employer makes concessional contributions to your super (such as the compulsory Superannuation Guarantee, currently 12%), those contributions are taxed at just 15% inside the fund. Concessional contributions are generally taxed at 15% in super. For people on higher marginal rates, that's lower than their income tax rate. The trade-off is that it's locked away until preservation age. Salary sacrifice arrangements — where you voluntarily contribute pre-tax income to super — use the same principle.

Tax on investment earnings inside super

Investment returns earned within a super fund — capital gains, dividends, interest — are also taxed at a maximum of 15% during the accumulation phase (while you're still working and building your balance). Capital gains on assets held for more than 12 months receive a one-third discount inside super, effectively reducing that rate to 10%.

Tax-free after 60

Once you reach age 60 and access your super through a retirement income stream, your withdrawals are generally tax-free. Investment earnings in a retirement-phase account are also tax-free, up to a transfer balance cap ($2.1 million in 2026–27, indexed to inflation). This is a significant benefit for long-term retirement planning.

How capital losses work

When you sell an investment for less than you paid for it, the difference is a capital loss. A capital loss is taken off any capital gains you made in the same financial year, which reduces the net capital gain that gets added to your income. A loss on paper doesn't count until the investment is actually sold.

For example, if someone made a $5,000 capital gain on one parcel of shares and sold another investment at a $2,000 loss in the same year, their net capital gain would be $3,000. Some people consider the timing of sales with this in mind; others decide what to sell on the investment's merits and treat the tax as a side effect.

  • Capital losses can only be used to offset capital gains, not ordinary income.
  • Unused capital losses can be carried forward to future tax years indefinitely.
  • There's also a wash sale risk — the ATO looks unfavourably on selling an asset purely to crystallise a loss and immediately buying it back to maintain the position. This may be treated as a scheme to avoid tax.

Tax planning, tax avoidance and tax evasion

These terms are often confused, but in Australia they have very different meanings and consequences.

Legitimate tax planning means using the rules as they're intended. Holding investments for more than 12 months and getting the CGT discount, making super contributions within the caps, and claiming franking credits are all examples of the system working as designed.

Tax avoidance schemes are different. In Australia, "tax avoidance" usually refers to arrangements set up mainly to get a tax benefit, even if each step looks legal on its own. The general anti-avoidance rules in Part IVA of the tax law let the ATO cancel the tax benefit from such a scheme and apply penalties.

Tax evasion is illegal. It involves deliberately concealing income, falsifying records, or lying to the ATO to reduce your tax obligation. Evasion can result in serious penalties, prosecution, and imprisonment. The ATO has significant powers to investigate and pursue tax evasion.

Paying less tax legally is the system working the way it was designed to.
