FIRE stories often feature high-income tech workers or dual-professional couples — which can make the whole idea feel irrelevant to someone on a typical income. It isn't. The maths still works. It just takes longer and needs a savings rate you can keep up.

Why income level matters less than people assume

The core formula (25 times your spending, reached through your savings rate) doesn't need a six-figure salary. A modest income with modest, deliberately-chosen spending and a realistic savings rate reaches the same kind of outcome as a higher income with higher spending; it just takes a proportionally longer or shorter time depending on the rate, not the raw dollar figure.

Common misconception

"FIRE requires a six-figure salary." It requires a meaningful gap between income and spending — which is achievable at many income levels, just via a different combination of savings rate and timeline.

A quick example

On a $75,000 salary, Ahmed saves 18% — a strong, sustainable rate for his situation, not an extreme one. That gap alone, invested consistently, projects to financial independence in his late 50s. It's not the "retire at 35" story from the FIRE forums, but it's a legitimate, achievable outcome on an ordinary income.

What a realistic timeline looks like

On an average income with a savings rate you can keep up (commonly 10-20% for many households), the timeline to financial independence is usually measured in decades rather than years. That's still a legitimate, achievable goal, just a longer one than the extreme FIRE case studies that dominate online.

An average income doesn't put early retirement out of reach. The plan just needs a pace that fits your income.
