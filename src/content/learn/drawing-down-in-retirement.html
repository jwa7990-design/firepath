Most money talk is about saving and investing. Far less is said about the other end: once you stop work, how do you turn what you've built into a pay packet that lasts? Here are the main ideas, in plain English.

The 4% rule, in practice

The 4% rule comes from US research on 30-year retirements. In practice it means this: in your first year, you take out 4% of what you've got. Each year after that, you take out the same dollar amount, bumped up for inflation, no matter what markets did.

So with $1,000,000 you'd take $40,000 in year one. If prices rise 3%, you'd take $41,200 the next year. You're slowly spending down your savings, while what's left keeps growing. Historically, that's lasted 30 years in the large majority of cases.

Flip it around and you get the freedom number: about 25 times your yearly spending.

Why early retirees often use 3.5%

Stop at 45 and your money might need to last 45 or 50 years, not 30. The longer the stretch, the more chance of hitting a rough patch you can't recover from. So many people retiring early plan around 3.5% instead, which means about 28.5 times spending. It's a bigger target, but it leaves more room for bad luck. Others stick with 4%, counting on being able to trim spending, earn a little, or lean on the Age Pension later. A bigger target can also mean working longer.

Sequence-of-returns risk

This is the big one. Sequence-of-returns risk means the order your returns arrive in matters once you're withdrawing.

If markets fall hard in your first few years of retirement, you're selling investments while they're down to pay the bills. Those sold units can't bounce back. Two people with the same average return over 30 years can end up in very different places, purely because one got the bad years early and the other got them late.

The first five to ten years of retirement are when this matters most. The next two ideas are ways people deal with it.

A cash buffer (or bucket approach)

A common approach is to keep one to three years of spending in cash, in a high-interest account or term deposits, with the rest invested for growth. In normal years you top up the cash from your investments. When markets fall, you live off the cash and leave your investments alone to recover.

Some people go further with a "bucket" setup: cash for the next couple of years, something steadier like bonds for the years after that, and growth investments for the long run. The trade-off is that cash earns less over time, so a big buffer can drag on long-term returns.

Common misconception

"Once I've picked a withdrawal rate, I'm locked in." Real retirees adjust. The 4% rule assumes you never change your spending, which is why it's fairly cautious. Being willing to flex makes a plan much sturdier.

Flexible spending

If you can trim spending in bad years (skip the overseas trip, put off the new car, eat out less), your money tends to last a lot longer. Even a 10% cut in a down year takes real pressure off your investments. Some people set simple rules ahead of time, like "if my balance drops more than 20%, we hold off on big extras until it recovers".

The bridge years, then super

In Australia you can generally get to your super from 60, once you've retired (or left a job after 60), or at 65 regardless. If you stop work earlier, you need money outside super to live on until then. These are the bridge years.

So an early retirement plan has two parts: enough outside super to cover the bridge, and enough in super to carry you from 60 on. Your super keeps growing untouched during the bridge years, which helps.

Super account-based pensions

From 60, many people move their super into an account-based pension: you keep the money invested and draw a regular income from it. A few rules apply:

  • Tax: from 60, withdrawals from a taxed super fund are tax-free. Investment earnings inside the pension are tax-free too, on up to $2.1 million moved into retirement phase (the 2026–27 transfer balance cap, which is indexed). Anything above that stays in accumulation, taxed at up to 15%.
  • Minimum drawdowns: you must take out at least a set percentage of your balance each year.

The Age Pension later on

From 67, the Age Pension may top up your income. It's means-tested on assets and income, and your home isn't counted. As your balance falls over retirement, you may qualify for a part pension, which can ease the load on your savings in your 70s and 80s.

Worked example

Kate, 50, is an IT project manager in Perth. She's ready to stop work with $450,000 invested outside super and $700,000 in super. She owns her home and spends $46,000 a year. That's 4% of her $1,150,000 total.

Bridge years (50 to 60): she draws $46,000 a year from her outside investments. Assuming they earn 4% a year after inflation, she'd have roughly $92,000 left at 60 (in today's dollars). She keeps about two years of spending of that pot in cash so a market fall early on doesn't force her to sell. Cash earns less, so in practice she'd end up with a bit less than that.

Super at 60: left alone and earning the same 4% after inflation, her super grows to about $1,036,000 in today's dollars. She starts an account-based pension. Her 4% minimum is about $41,000, under what she spends, so she tops up from her remaining outside money as needed. All of it is tax-free.

From 67: as a single homeowner with around $1 million, she's over the $745,750 assets test cut-off, so no Age Pension at first. If her balance drops over time, a part pension could start later.

Real returns won't arrive in a neat 4% each year. A bad run in her first few years would hurt most, which is why her cash buffer and willingness to trim spending matter.

Things to weigh up

  • How many years might your money need to last?
  • How much of your spending could you trim in a bad year?
  • How big a cash buffer would let you sleep at night, given it earns less?
  • How much do you have outside super for the bridge years before 60?
  • How comfortable are you with market ups and downs once you're living off your savings?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.
