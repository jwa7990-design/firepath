Once you've got a home loan and some spare cash, a question turns up: does that money go into the offset account, or into shares? One option gives you a sure thing. The other gives you a shot at more. Which comes out ahead depends on three numbers: your mortgage rate, your tax rate and what you expect investing to return.

How an offset account works

An offset account is a transaction account linked to your home loan. Each day, your lender subtracts the offset balance from what you owe before working out interest. If you owe $500,000 and have $40,000 in the offset, you're charged interest on $460,000.

So every dollar in the offset saves interest at your mortgage rate. At 6%, that's 6 cents per dollar per year. Two things make that saving stand out:

  • It's guaranteed. As long as the money's there, you save the interest. No market can take it away.
  • It's tax-free. Interest you avoid paying isn't income, so there's no tax on it. On your own home loan, the interest isn't tax-deductible either, so every dollar saved is a full dollar.

The after-tax comparison

To compare fairly, you need the return from investing after tax, set against the mortgage rate. Dividends and interest from investments are taxed at your marginal rate (the rate on your next dollar of income), plus the 2% Medicare levy. For 2026–27, that's 32% for income between $45,001 and $135,000, and 39% between $135,001 and $190,000.

The question becomes: what return would investing need, before tax, to match a 6% saving that's tax-free?

Your tax rate (incl. Medicare) If all of the return is taxed each year If all of it is long-term growth with the CGT discount
32% (30% bracket) About 8.8% About 7.1%
39% (37% bracket) About 9.8% About 7.5%

Break-even returns needed to match a 6% mortgage rate.

Real share returns sit somewhere between those two columns. Part comes as dividends, taxed each year, though Australian shares often carry franking credits that reduce the tax. Part comes as growth, which isn't taxed until you sell, and only half the gain is taxed if you've held for over 12 months (the CGT discount). So depending on the mix, the break-even at a 6% mortgage rate lands somewhere around 7–10% a year before tax.

Diversified shares have historically returned roughly 7–10% a year before tax and inflation over long periods, with plenty of bad years along the way. That's right in the break-even zone. At a 6% mortgage rate, the expected edge from investing is thin, and it comes with risk the offset doesn't have.

Worked example Daniel, 41, is an electrician in Perth earning $110,000 a year, which puts him in the 30% bracket (32% with the Medicare levy). He has $40,000 spare and a home loan at an example rate of 6%.

In the offset: he saves $2,400 a year in interest. No tax, no risk.

Invested at 7% a year, with all of it taxed as income: $2,800 before tax, about $1,904 after tax. He'd be about $500 a year behind the offset.

If Daniel earned over $135,000 and paid 39%, the same 7% would leave about $1,708, roughly $700 behind. To come out ahead, his investments would need to earn more, or more of the return would need to come as long-term growth taxed at the discounted rate.
Common misconception

"Shares return more than my mortgage rate, so investing always wins." That compares a before-tax, uncertain return against an after-tax, guaranteed one. Once tax is counted, a 7% share return can leave you behind a 6% offset.

The answer moves with rates and tax

Mortgage rates follow the RBA cash rate up and down, so the 6% here is only an example. When rates are lower, say 4%, the offset saves less and the break-even return drops to roughly 5–6.5% before tax. Investing then has a much wider margin to work with. When rates are higher, the offset gets harder to beat.

Your tax bracket matters too. The higher your marginal rate, the more a tax-free offset saving is worth compared with taxed investment income. A pay rise that moves you into the 37% bracket shifts the maths towards the offset. A drop to part-time work does the opposite.

Getting to your money: offset vs redraw

Both an offset and extra repayments with a redraw facility save interest at the mortgage rate. The difference is access and flexibility.

  • Offset: the money is still yours, in a normal account. You can move it any time without asking the lender.
  • Redraw: the extra repayments become part of the loan. Lenders can set minimum redraw amounts, charge fees, or reduce or freeze redraw access.

There's a tax angle too. If you ever turn the home into an investment property, money sitting in an offset can be taken out without changing the loan. Paying the loan down and then redrawing for a new purpose can affect how much of the interest is tax-deductible. That's a question for a tax agent before you act.

Shares can be sold within a few days, but you might have to sell at a bad time and pay capital gains tax when you do.

Risk and timing

The offset gives the same saving every year. Investing gives an average, and averages hide a lot. Shares can drop 30% or more in a bad year, and it can take years to recover.

That matters most when the timing goes against you. If markets fall just as you lose a job or need cash for a renovation, you'd be selling at the worst moment. Money in an offset doesn't have that problem.

On the other side, investing gives you diversification. With everything in the offset, all your wealth sits in one house. Investments outside your home can also produce income for your freedom number, while home equity mostly doesn't unless you sell or downsize.

The core trade-off: the offset gives a certain, tax-free saving at your mortgage rate. Investing gives a higher expected return with more risk, taxed along the way. At a 6% rate the expected gap is small. The lower your rate and your tax bracket, the more room investing has.

Doing a bit of both

This doesn't have to be all or nothing. Plenty of people keep their emergency fund and a buffer in the offset, then invest regular amounts on top. Others go hard on the offset while rates are high and switch to investing as the loan shrinks or rates fall. Super is another option, with its own tax rules and access age.

The right mix depends on your income, how secure your job is, how long until you'd need the money, and how you'd feel watching investments fall. A licensed financial adviser can look at your full situation if you'd like help weighing it up.

An offset is a guaranteed, tax-free saving at your mortgage rate. Investing only comes out ahead once it beats that after tax, and that line moves every time rates or your tax bracket do.
