If you've got a HECS or HELP debt (the government loan that covers uni fees), you've probably wondered whether to throw spare cash at it or invest instead. The rules changed a fair bit over the last few years, so a lot of the advice floating around is out of date. Here's how it works for 2026–27.

How the debt grows: indexation

A HELP debt doesn't charge interest the way a credit card or home loan does. Instead, once a year on 1 June, the ATO adds indexation: it bumps up the part of your balance that's been owing for more than 11 months, so the debt keeps pace with prices or wages.

Since a law change passed in late 2024, the rate is the lower of two measures: inflation (the Consumer Price Index, or CPI) or wages growth (the Wage Price Index, or WPI). The change was backdated to 1 June 2023, so the big 7.1% jump that year was cut to 3.2%, and the ATO credited the difference back to people's accounts.

In recent years indexation has sat at around 3–4%. The government publishes each year's rate before 1 June, and that's the rate your debt grows at if you leave it alone.

The 20% cut. Parliament passed a one-off 20% reduction in mid-2025. The ATO took 20% off everyone's HELP and other student loan balances as they stood on 1 June 2025, before that year's indexation was added. If you had a debt then, it's already been applied. You don't need to do anything.

How compulsory repayments work now

From 2025–26, compulsory repayments switched to a marginal system. You only repay on the income above the threshold, a bit like tax brackets. The old system took a percentage of your whole income once you crossed a line, which made for some nasty jumps.

For 2026–27, based on your repayment income (taxable income plus things like salary sacrificed super):

Repayment incomeCompulsory repayment
Up to $69,528Nothing
$69,529 – $129,71715c for each $1 over $69,528
$129,718 – $186,050$9,028 plus 17c for each $1 over $129,717
Over $186,05010% of your total repayment income

These thresholds are indexed each year. The repayment comes out through your pay (your employer withholds it) and gets squared up at tax time.

One thing that trips people up: the compulsory repayment depends on your income, not your balance. Paying a chunk off early doesn't lower what comes out of your pay. It just means the debt is gone sooner, and then those repayments stop.

Common misconception

"Voluntary repayments get a bonus." They used to: a 5% or 10% bonus on voluntary repayments. That ended on 1 January 2017. Today a $1,000 voluntary payment takes exactly $1,000 off your debt.

The real comparison: indexation vs an after-tax return

Here's the core of it. Every dollar you pay off saves you the indexation on that dollar. So paying off HELP is like earning the indexation rate, recently around 3%, with no tax and no market risk.

Investing the same dollar might earn more. Diversified shares have historically returned somewhere around 7% a year before inflation over the long run, though some years are well below that and some years are negative. And investment returns are taxed, while the indexation you avoid isn't.

So the fair comparison is indexation (often close to inflation) against what you'd keep after tax from investing. Over past long periods, diversified shares have returned more than recent indexation rates, but that's not guaranteed, and it comes with ups and downs along the way.

Worked example

Mia, 29, is a physio in Adelaide earning $85,000. She has a $20,000 HELP balance and $10,000 sitting in savings that she doesn't need for anything else.

Her compulsory repayment for 2026–27 is about $2,321 (15c on each dollar over $69,528). At that pace, with indexation around 3%, her debt would be gone in about 10 years. If she puts the $10,000 on it now, it's gone in about 5.

Option A: pay $10,000 off HELP. At 3% indexation, that saves about $1,590 of indexation over five years. That's a sure thing, give or take how indexation moves.

Option B: invest the $10,000. Say it earns 7% a year. Mia's marginal tax rate is 32% (30% plus the 2% Medicare levy). If all of the return were taxed each year, she'd keep about 4.8%, so after five years she'd have roughly $12,620, about $1,030 more than Option A. In practice, part of a share return is growth that isn't taxed until you sell, so she might keep a bit more. But in a bad five years she could end up with less than she put in.

Neither choice changes her pay packet for now, because her compulsory repayment stays at $2,321 a year either way until the debt's cleared.

What about borrowing for a home?

This is where HELP matters more than its small indexation rate suggests. When you apply for a home loan, lenders count your compulsory HELP repayment as a cost coming out of your income. A bigger repayment means less income left over, which can lower how much they'll lend you.

In Mia's case, $2,321 a year is about $190 a month off what a lender sees as available income. Lenders each have their own approach, and some may look past a debt that's nearly paid off, so some people ask any lender they're talking to how they treat it. If you're planning to buy in the next year or two, clearing a small balance can make a difference that the indexation maths alone won't show.

The trade-offs of paying it off early

  1. The money's locked in. Once you pay it, you can't get it back. Cash in savings or investments stays available for an emergency or a house deposit.
  2. The saving is small but certain. You avoid indexation, recently around 3%, with no risk. Investing might earn more but could also earn less.
  3. Timing around 1 June. Indexation is added on 1 June, so a voluntary payment made before then avoids that year's increase on the amount you pay.
  4. Peace of mind counts. Some people just like being rid of it. That's a fair reason, as long as you know what you're trading.
  5. Cash flow stays the same until it's gone. Your pay packet only goes up once the whole balance is cleared.

Some people prefer to pay it down early for the certainty, the borrowing boost or the relief of being rid of it. Others prefer to invest or keep cash, because the indexation rate is low and the money stays available. The right mix depends on your income, your plans for a home, how much buffer you have and how you feel about market ups and downs.

Things to weigh up

  • Are you planning to apply for a home loan in the next year or two?
  • How much buffer would you have left if you paid a chunk off?
  • How would you feel if investments fell in the years straight after?
  • How much is being debt-free worth to you, beyond the numbers?

Everyone’s situation is different. A licensed financial adviser can help you weigh these up for your circumstances.
