How Do I Calculate My Own Retirement Age?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

Every FIRE calculator, including FirePath, runs on the same underlying formula. Here it is broken into steps you could genuinely do by hand.

Step one: find your target number

Work out your expected annual spending in retirement, then multiply by 25. That's your freedom number — the portfolio size that, at a roughly 4% withdrawal rate, could sustain that spending indefinitely. For the full reasoning behind this figure, How Much Money Do I Actually Need to Retire in Australia? covers it directly.

Step two: know your starting point and rate

You need your current invested savings (excluding super, which runs on its own timeline) and how much you're adding to that pool each month.

Common misconception

"This requires complicated financial modelling." It doesn't. It's four numbers — spending, current savings, monthly contribution, and an assumed return — run through one compounding formula. The complexity people imagine isn't there.

A quick example

Grace wants to spend $55,000 a year, giving a $1.375 million target. She has $95,000 saved and invests $900 a month. At a 7% return, compounding that forward month by month crosses $1.375 million at roughly age 57, starting from 34. That's the entire calculation — done by hand, in a few lines.

Step three: apply a return assumption and project forward

Using a long-term real (after-inflation) return assumption — commonly 7% for a diversified share portfolio — you compound your starting balance and contributions forward month by month until the total crosses your target number. The month that happens is your projected retirement date.

Why doing this by hand teaches you something a calculator doesn't

Once you've worked through it manually even once, it becomes obvious which levers actually move the date: a higher savings rate moves it a lot; a slightly higher return assumption moves it a little; reduced planned spending moves it on both ends at once (smaller target, same contributions).

A calculator does this instantly. But once you understand why the number moves, you stop needing to trust it blindly.
