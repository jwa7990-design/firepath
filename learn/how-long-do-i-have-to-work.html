How long do you actually have to work for?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

Ask most people how long they have to work for, and they'll give you an age, not a number they've actually calculated. "Until 67, I guess." "Whenever the pension kicks in." "As long as I have to." Very few people have ever sat down and worked out the real answer — because the real answer isn't a fixed age at all. It's a maths problem, and the inputs are almost entirely things you control.

The Question You're Actually Asking

"How long do I have to work for" and "how long until I turn 67" are two completely different questions, even though most people treat them as the same one. The second question is about a government-set eligibility age. The first is about financial independence — the point where your investments generate enough income that paid work becomes optional, not compulsory.

Those two numbers can be wildly different. Someone with a high savings rate might reach financial independence in their 40s, decades before pension eligibility. Someone with a 0% savings rate may never truly reach it, regardless of age, because there's no invested capital generating income to replace their wage.

The Formula Behind the Number

The core maths comes from what's widely known as the 4% rule, drawn from historical market research (originally the Trinity Study in the US, since revisited many times). It suggests that if you withdraw around 4% of your portfolio per year, adjusted for inflation, your money has a strong historical likelihood of lasting 30+ years — often indefinitely, since market growth frequently outpaces the withdrawal rate.

Flip that percentage around and you get the number FIRE calculators actually use: 25 times your annual spending. If you spend $60,000 a year, your "freedom number" is roughly $1.5 million. That's the amount of invested wealth that could theoretically replace your income indefinitely.

The part people miss: your freedom number is driven by your spending, not your income. Someone earning $200,000 who spends $180,000 of it needs a much bigger number than someone earning $80,000 who spends $40,000. Income determines how fast you can get there — spending determines how far away "there" actually is.

Why Savings Rate Changes Everything

Here's the number that surprises almost everyone the first time they see it: at a normal savings rate, most people are working for 40+ years. At a high savings rate, that number can fall dramatically — not because they're earning more, but because the gap between what they earn and what they spend does two jobs at once. It builds the pile, and it shrinks the target, since a lower spending rate means a smaller number is needed to cover it.

These figures assume a 7% real (after-inflation) investment return and no starting balance — they're illustrative, not a guarantee, since real returns vary year to year. But the shape of the curve is the real lesson here: the relationship between savings rate and years-to-freedom isn't a straight line. It curves sharply, which means the jump from 15% to 25% buys you more freed-up years than the jump from 40% to 50% does. Small increases from a low base matter enormously.

What Actually Moves the Number

Since the answer isn't a fixed age, it means the number is genuinely responsive to decisions you make. A few things that move it more than people expect:

  • An extra $50 a week saved and invested can shave meaningful time off the timeline — often more than people assume, because of compounding.
  • Reducing planned retirement spending shrinks your target number directly — every dollar less you plan to spend annually in retirement is 25 fewer dollars you need to have saved.
  • Where your money sits matters as much as how much you save. Cash sitting in a low-interest account compounds far slower than money invested in line with long-term market returns.
  • Debt — particularly high-interest debt — actively works against you, since it's compounding in the opposite direction.

The Australian Complication: Two Timelines, Not One

For Australians, this question actually has two answers running in parallel, because of how superannuation works. Your super is generally locked until your preservation age (60 for most people currently), and grows via employer contributions and investment returns the whole time, regardless of what you do with your other savings.

This means your real timeline is really: how long until my accessible savings and investments (outside super) can cover my spending, until my super becomes available to take over? For some people, this creates a genuine gap to plan for — a period after they've stopped earning a wage but before super kicks in. For others, particularly those retiring closer to or after 60, the two timelines converge and the calculation is simpler.

You Might Be Closer Than You Think

There's a version of financial independence that doesn't require hitting the full number at all. It's called Coast FIRE — the point where your current invested savings, left alone with no further contributions, will compound to your full freedom number by a normal retirement age purely through investment growth. If you've reached Coast FIRE, you technically don't need to save anything more for retirement specifically — you could redirect that money elsewhere, work fewer hours, or take a lower-paying but more enjoyable job, and your future is still on track.

Most people have never checked whether they've already reached this point. It's often much closer than people assume, especially for those in their 30s and 40s who started investing early.

The Honest Answer

How long you have to work for isn't a fact about you — it's a calculation based on your specific income, spending, savings rate, and what your money is invested in. Two people the same age, on the same salary, can have completely different answers depending on decisions that are, for the most part, within their control.

The only way to actually know your number is to calculate it with your real figures — not a rule of thumb, not "until 67," your actual number.
