How Do I Know If I'm On Track for Retirement?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

"On track" gets used constantly and defined rarely. Most people who ask this question have never run the numbers — they're going on a feeling, usually driven by anxiety rather than evidence.

Defining "on track" properly

Being on track means: at your current savings rate, invested at a reasonable long-term return, you'll reach a portfolio of roughly 25 times your planned retirement spending by the age you want to stop working. That's it — three inputs (savings rate, current balance, target spending) and one comparison (does the maths land on your desired timeline).

Common misconception

"If I'm saving something every month, I must be on track." Saving *something* and saving *enough for your specific timeline* are different things. The only way to know which one is true is to actually run the projection.

A quick example

Sarah is 42. She has $120,000 invested and saves $700 a month. She wants $70,000 a year in retirement, which puts her freedom number at $1.75 million. Projected forward at a 7% return, her current savings rate gets her there at 61 — five years later than the 56 she was hoping for. Now she knows the actual gap, not just a vague sense that "it'll probably be fine."

The three-question self-check

Ask yourself: What do I spend per year? What's my current savings rate? What age do I want to stop working? Run those three answers through the 25x-spending formula and a compounding projection, and you get a real year — not a feeling. If your number already looks strong, it's worth checking What Is Coast FIRE too, since you may be further along than you think.

What to do if the answer is "not yet"

Discovering you're not on track for your ideal timeline isn't a failure — it's information. The two levers that move the number are increasing your savings rate and adjusting your planned spending. Even a modest increase, sustained over years, shifts the outcome more than people expect because of compounding.

"On track" isn't a feeling you carry around. It's a calculation you can actually run — and once you run it, the anxiety usually shrinks to fit the real gap.
