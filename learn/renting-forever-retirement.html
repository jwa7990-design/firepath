Does Renting Forever Ruin Your Retirement?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

Renting long-term into retirement carries real, specific risks — but "ruined" is too strong a word for a plan that's simply built differently to the traditional own-your-home model.

The genuine risk worth naming honestly

Rent tends to rise over time, and unlike a paid-off mortgage, there's no point where housing costs drop to near-zero in retirement. This means a renting-forever retirement plan needs a larger investment portfolio to sustainably cover ongoing rent indefinitely, compared to someone who owns their home outright by retirement.

Common misconception

"You can't retire comfortably if you rent forever." You can — but your freedom number needs to explicitly include rent as an ongoing cost, not assume it disappears the way a mortgage eventually does.

A quick example

Two retirees plan to spend $50,000 a year. One owns their home outright, so that $50,000 excludes housing. The other plans to rent, and needs to add $22,000 a year in rent on top — making their real target $72,000, and their freedom number 25x that: $1.8 million versus $1.25 million. Same lifestyle otherwise, very different required portfolio, purely because one plan forgot to explicitly include rent.

What needs to be true for this to work

What needs to be true for this to work: your retirement number (25x spending) needs to explicitly include realistic ongoing rent as part of that annual spending figure — not just living costs excluding housing.

Why this gets missed

Many retirement calculations implicitly assume home ownership by retirement (rent excluded from spending, mortgage paid off). If renting is genuinely the long-term plan, that assumption needs to be explicitly corrected — otherwise the calculated freedom number will be too low for the real cost of living being planned for. For the broader cost comparison, Is Renting Cheaper Than Owning Long-Term? covers the full picture.

Renting forever doesn't ruin retirement — it just requires a bigger, more explicitly-calculated portfolio to fund it sustainably.
