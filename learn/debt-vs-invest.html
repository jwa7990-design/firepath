Should I Pay Off Debt or Invest First?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

One number decides this whole question: the interest rate on your debt. Not your personality, not whether you're "a saver or a spender" — just that one figure, compared against what investing would realistically return instead.

The actual decision rule

If your debt's interest rate is higher than the return you'd reasonably expect from investing (commonly estimated around 7% real return for diversified shares over the long term), paying off that debt first is mathematically the stronger move — it's a guaranteed "return" equal to the interest rate you stop paying. If the debt's rate is lower than that, investing alongside it can make sense.

Common misconception

"All debt should be paid off before investing." Not all debt is equal. A 20% credit card and a 4% mortgage deserve completely different treatment — lumping them together as "debt" hides the actual decision that matters.

A quick example

Ryan has $8,000 on a credit card at 19% interest, and a mortgage at 5.8%. Paying down the credit card first is close to a guaranteed 19% "return" — nothing realistic beats that. His mortgage, at 5.8%, sits closer to expected long-term investment returns, making "invest alongside it" a genuinely reasonable choice rather than a mistake.

Where it gets more nuanced

Lower-interest debt — a mortgage, or HECS/HELP (which is indexed rather than charged commercial interest) — sits in a different category. Many people reasonably choose to invest alongside these debts rather than aggressively paying them off early, since the "return" from eliminating them is lower than realistic investment growth over time.

The exception worth naming

Purely mathematically optimal isn't the only valid consideration — some people place real value on being debt-free for peace of mind, even where the numbers would favour investing instead. That's a legitimate personal choice, not a financial error, as long as it's made knowingly rather than by default.

The rule of thumb: high-interest debt first, low-interest debt is more genuinely a judgement call.
