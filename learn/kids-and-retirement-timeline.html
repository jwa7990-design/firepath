How Does Having Kids Change Your Retirement Timeline?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

Kids genuinely change household finances — but the framing matters. This is a real cost worth planning for honestly, not a guilt trip about whether having children was "financially responsible."

The real, temporary cost

Childcare, education, and general costs of raising children do reduce the amount available to save and invest during those years — often quite substantially while children are young, particularly if formal childcare is involved. This is a genuine, calculable dent in the savings rate during that period.

Common misconception

"Having kids permanently ruins your FIRE timeline." The heaviest costs are concentrated in specific years, not spread evenly across your whole working life. The timeline slows during those years and then often speeds back up.

A quick example

Before their first child, Mia and Alex were saving 30% of their income, on track to retire at 47. During their children's early years, formal childcare and reduced hours dropped that to 8% for about six years. Once both kids were in school and one parent increased hours again, their savings rate climbed back to 22% — pushing their projected retirement from 47 to 53. A real delay, but a specific, calculable one, not an open-ended derailment.

How to model it honestly

Rather than assuming a single constant savings rate across your whole working life, a more realistic projection lowers the assumed rate during the higher-cost child-rearing years and raises it again afterward. This gives a more accurate timeline than either ignoring the cost entirely or assuming it lasts forever.

Supports worth factoring in

Family Tax Benefit and other government supports can meaningfully offset some costs depending on eligibility — worth checking current criteria via Services Australia rather than assuming a fixed figure, since thresholds change. If you're planning this as a single-income household specifically, How Do I Retire Early as a Single Parent? covers that angle directly.

Having kids moves the timeline — it doesn't erase the plan, and the numbers usually improve again sooner than people expect.
