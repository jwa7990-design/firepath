Can I Afford to Work Less?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

Here's a number worth sitting with: some people asking this question have already reached the point where the answer is yes, and have no idea. This question usually shows up during burnout, not during careful financial planning — which is exactly why it deserves a proper calculation rather than an anxious guess in either direction.

The concept that actually answers this: Coast FIRE

If your current invested savings, left completely alone with no further contributions, would compound to your full retirement number by a normal retirement age purely through investment growth, you've reached what's called Coast FIRE. At that point, you technically don't need to keep saving for retirement specifically — freeing up income to redirect toward reduced hours, a career change, or simply breathing room. The full mechanics are in What Is Coast FIRE?

Common misconception

"Working less means giving up on retirement." Not necessarily. If you've already reached Coast FIRE, reducing hours doesn't threaten your eventual retirement at all — it only changes your income and lifestyle right now, which is a separate, more immediate calculation.

A quick example

Marcus, 39, has $310,000 invested and a $1.4 million freedom number at his planned retirement age of 60. Projected at 7% with zero further contributions, his current balance alone reaches that target by 59 — meaning he's essentially already Coast FIRE. Dropping to four days a week wouldn't change his eventual retirement date at all; it would only change what the next twenty years feel like.

How to model it

Take your current invested balance (excluding super, since that's a separate locked timeline), and project it forward at a reasonable long-term return to your target retirement age. If it already reaches your 25x-spending number without another dollar added, working less doesn't threaten your eventual retirement — it just changes your income and lifestyle right now, which is a completely separate, more immediate calculation (can your reduced income cover your reduced-hours cost of living).

What if you haven't reached it yet?

Working less is still possible — it just means your retirement timeline moves later, in direct proportion to how much less you're contributing. That's not necessarily the wrong trade. It's simply a trade worth seeing clearly, in real years, rather than guessing at.

The question isn't really "can I afford to work less" — it's "what does working less actually cost me, in years, and am I okay with that number."
