How Much Super Will I Have at 60?

Part of the FirePath Learning Lab · Educational only, not financial advice · Browse all topics →

Projecting a super balance decades into the future is uncertain — but the mechanics of the estimate are straightforward, even if the exact final number can't be guaranteed.

The three inputs that drive the projection

Your current super balance, ongoing employer Super Guarantee contributions (currently a set percentage of your gross income, paid on top of your salary), and an assumed long-term investment return (commonly modelled around 7% before the concessional tax treatment inside super) — projected forward, compounding, to age 60.

Common misconception

"My super balance today roughly shows what I'll retire with." Not close — compounding means the bulk of your eventual balance is typically built in the final 10-15 years before retirement, not evenly across your career.

A quick example

At 35, with $85,000 in super and ongoing Super Guarantee contributions on a $90,000 salary, projecting forward at 7% to age 60 lands around $520,000 — more than six times the current balance, almost entirely from 25 years of compounding on contributions that feel modest in any single year.

Why employer contributions add up more than expected

Employer contributions alone add up more than most people expect over a full working life — the Super Guarantee is one of the more effective forced-savings mechanisms available, precisely because it happens automatically regardless of discipline.

Why this is an estimate, not a guarantee

Investment returns vary year to year and won't follow a smooth average in reality — some years will be strongly positive, others negative. Career breaks, job changes, and voluntary contributions (or lack of them) also shift the real outcome from any straight-line projection. Most super funds provide a projection tool using your actual balance and contribution history — a more accurate starting point than any generic estimate.
